The Competitive Briefing Template

A fixed output schema that turns raw multi-source research into a document a product manager can act on in one read — not a report to re-read for the decision buried inside it.

Companion toolRadar — competitive intelligence agent
Format6-part structured schema (Pydantic)
Applies toAny company, any competitor set
Version1.0
Brief

Most "competitive analysis" output is prose a reader has to mine for the actual decision. This template inverts that: every section is designed backward from a question a PM actually asks — what's the landscape, what can I copy, what's coming for me, what do I do about it — and every field has a rule that forces the model to answer with a specific, sourced claim instead of a plausible-sounding summary.

It was designed alongside the agent that fills it, not after — the schema is the prompt contract. Six sections, each below with its purpose, its field-level rule, and a specimen pulled from a real run of the tool (not a mockup).

Executive Summary

field: executive_summary — string, 2–3 paragraphs

The one section written for someone who will never open the other five. States who the real competitors are and what the landscape's shape is — not a recap of what follows it.

Rule: 2–3 paragraphs, no bullet list. Must name the actual competitor set in the first sentence — never open with a generic "the market is competitive" line.

"The competitive landscape for productivity and project management tools in 2025 is highly dynamic, with Notion facing significant competition from Trello, Asana, ClickUp, and Coda. Each competitor offers unique strengths and caters to different user needs… Trello is known for its simplicity and Kanban-style project management, appealing to marketing and operations teams. Asana, on the other hand, is favored by project managers for its robust task management and timeline features…"

Feature Comparison Matrix

field: feature_comparison — 5–8 rows × {feature, [competitor, level]}

The at-a-glance table a prose summary can't be. Every row is a feature that actually appeared in research — the model can't invent categories to fill space.

Rule: level is exactly one of Yes / Partial / No. "No" is only assigned when research gives no evidence a feature exists — absence isn't assumed.

Competitor Profile

field: pricing / key_features / market_positioning / target_audience / recent_developments — each {content, sources[]}

Five fixed dimensions per competitor. Each is sourced by construction — the schema requires 3–5 citations per dimension, so a claim without a URL behind it can't reach the page.

Rule: sources come only from that dimension's own research — never borrowed from a different dimension to pad an empty list. A dimension with nothing to cite ships with an honest empty list, not a filler link.

"Airtable offers a range of pricing plans starting with a free version that supports up to 5 editors and 1,000 records per base. The Team plan costs $20 per user per month when billed annually, while the Business plan is priced at $45 per user per month…"

— "Airtable's paid plans start from $20 per user per month and go up to $45 per user per month"
thedigitalprojectmanager.com/tools/airtable-pricing
— "Airtable pricing ranges from Free to $45/editor/month on Business plans"
noloco.io/blog/airtable-pricing
Ships

"$11.99/mo for Prime, $14.99 standalone; includes HD audio and offline downloads."

Rejected

"Outlines the pricing plans available to users."

04
Defense

Key Threats

field: key_threats — string[3–5] per competitor

Names the exact mechanism, not the vibe. A threat must say what specific lever the competitor is pulling and what it blocks — never "has a large user base."

Ships

"Airtable's per-editor billing escalates fast for teams with many occasional contributors — competitors can't match without the same cost structure."

Rejected

"Airtable's pricing could hurt our competitiveness."

05
Offense

Opportunities

field: opportunities — string[3–5] per competitor

The mirror of 04: names the competitor's specific gap and what to build to exploit it — not "we should innovate more."

"Airtable lacks a strong frontend or app-building platform, requiring third-party tools for client-facing applications — Notion could capitalize by enhancing its own app-building capabilities."

06
Decision

Recommended Actions

field: recommended_actions — {action, impact 1–5, effort 1–5, rationale}

Where the briefing stops describing the landscape and starts prioritizing a roadmap. Every action is scored, not just listed — so it sorts straight into a quadrant instead of a flat to-do list.

Design principles behind the schema
§
Sourced by construction, not by request.

Every dimension's schema requires a sources array — a model can't emit a claim without also emitting where it came from, because the field doesn't validate otherwise.

§
Paired good/bad examples, not adjectives.

"Be specific" is not a rule a model can check itself against. A concrete GOOD/BAD pair per field is — it's the difference between a briefing and a mad-lib.

§
Score the recommendation, don't just make it.

A list of ideas isn't a roadmap input. Impact/effort scoring is what lets "recommended actions" sort into a decision instead of a wishlist.

§
An empty field is a valid answer.

When research turns up nothing for a dimension, the template ships an honest empty sources list — not a borrowed link dressed up to look sourced.
